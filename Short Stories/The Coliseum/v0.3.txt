The Crumbling Coliseum

Night after night, I find myself drifting toward the shadows, chasing something valuable—something I can’t name. I circle the old warrior’s coliseum as if its ruins hold the buried treasure I’ve spent my life seeking. Is tonight the night I unearth it? Perhaps.

I peer through the warped window, its edges jagged like broken teeth, into the coliseum’s heart. Noble columns, once blazing with light and triumph, now sit in mournful ruin. Deep cracks spread across their surfaces like scars carved by unseen hands, and the cold midnight sky brushes them with indifference.

Beneath my feet, the dust shifts with every step. It clings to my boots, coats my hands when I steady myself against the crumbling walls, and catches in the wind as if trying to flee this place. This dust—it’s all that remains of something greater, fragments of dreams reduced to powder. Every grain once belonged to something whole: the mortar that held the walls, the ceiling that crowned the coliseum, the statues that inspired awe. Now it’s scattered and weightless, too insubstantial to rebuild with and yet too heavy to ignore. I wonder how much of myself is in this dust, how much of what I once was has been ground into nothing but debris.

How does something so magnificent, so unshakable, fall to ruin? Generations labored to raise it from the dust, and now, in the blink of a lifetime, it crumbles. I can’t help but wonder: was it inevitable? Or was its destruction my doing?

In its golden days, the coliseum was a sanctuary where the walls erased division. Noblemen laughed with peasants; the rich broke bread with the starving. Here, for fleeting moments, everyone was simply human. We gathered not as strangers but as equals, breathing the same air, sharing the same dreams. I remember standing among them once, feeling the pulse of belonging. And now… I stand alone.

The columns tell a story, each one shaped by the hands of a different artist. No two are the same—one carved with jagged spirals of grief, another etched with the delicate latticework of love turned to ash. Some are marked with scenes of triumph, warriors frozen in stone mid-victory, their faces chipped away by time. Others bear softer designs: the outstretched hands of lovers caught in their final embrace or the branches of a tree winding up the column, its leaves now eroded into faint shadows.

One column is different—smooth and unadorned, its surface untouched by the artist’s hand. I pause in front of it, wondering why it was left blank. Perhaps its creator had too much to say and could never settle on the right design. Or maybe it was meant to hold something fragile, something too precious to carve in stone. Whatever the reason, it stands out among its brethren, as lonely and silent as the ruins around it.

The stones beneath my feet are the debris of what once held the sky. These shards and broken fragments were once ceilings, soaring high above the throngs of people who gathered here. Now, they’ve fallen—just rubble underfoot, discarded, forgotten. As I step deeper into the ruins, I can almost hear the clash of swords echoing through the hollow halls. The walls still bear the stains of blood spilled in honor of distant kings, their faces long forgotten.

Were these battles for glory? For survival? Or for nothing at all? I wonder, were these walls ever strong enough to hold such violence—and was I?

In the center of the coliseum, I pause. My breath catches as the ghosts of its prime rise around me. I can hear the roaring crowd, their cheers shaking the air. Fighters move through their stances, blades gleaming under the sun’s gaze. Blacksmiths work tirelessly, sparks flying as they prepare tools for victory—or for defeat. And somewhere, a king speaks, not as a tyrant but as an equal, his words uniting rather than dividing.

But then the ghosts fade, leaving only silence. The crowd is gone. The fighters have disappeared. Only the broken stones remain.

I glance back toward the window—the odd, lonely window carved into this place of stone. Why is it here, in a building of walls and bricks? It feels out of place, as if it shouldn’t exist. One glance through it reveals everything, and yet it hides more than it shows.

Still, I like where the window is. I can’t explain it, but there’s something strangely beautiful about its placement, like it was meant to be a grand bay window, a place to let the light in. But even as I admire it, I can’t help but feel it’s utterly useless. No light reaches inside this coliseum anymore. The glass is gone, the frame warped, and the view it offers is more unsettling than comforting.

What’s worse, this window wasn’t always here. It was carved into the wall, a deep wound in the stone, and I allowed it to happen. I remember the hands of others chipping away at it, their tools sharp and unrelenting. They promised it would let the world in, that it would bring something beautiful to the coliseum’s darkened halls. And I believed them. I stood aside and let them take their hammers and chisels to the wall, not realizing what I was giving up. Now, it’s here—this window, this hole in the fortress I built for myself. It stands as proof of my hope, my vulnerability, and the damage left behind when their promises proved empty.

Once, a museum came with grand promises to restore this place. They arrived with banners and tools, eager to sweep away the rubble and raise the coliseum from its knees. But they misunderstood it. They stripped away the fragments that gave it meaning, leaving it emptier than before. When their gold ran dry, they vanished, abandoning their half-built illusions and leaving behind a ruin that no longer recognized itself.

It wasn’t just one group. There were many—a long line of supposed restorers, each claiming to represent the museum, each armed with their own tools and blueprints. Some worked on the walls, chiseling away the cracks only to deepen them. Others labored over the columns, swearing they could reinforce them, but instead left them more unstable than before. One even spent months in the center of the coliseum, claiming they would restore the foundation itself, only to abandon the project and leave me with a gaping hole. Each one left me worse than they found me, until I stopped hoping for restoration at all.

I can’t help but sympathize with the coliseum. Thousands of pounds of gold and silver, thousands of miles crossed for materials—everything poured into its creation. The greatest architects of their time bled and toiled to raise something that would stand against the march of time. And for a while, it worked. This was a place where fears were laid to rest, sorrows eased, and hungry mouths fed. But in the blink of an eye, it was reduced to rubble and ash.

Taken up by a favored few, promising to restore it to its former glory, they left it more broken than before. Now the columns stand for no one and for no reason. Their origin is forgotten, their purpose erased.

But that’s not entirely true, is it? I’m still here, standing in the center of it all, trying to make sense of the rubble. There’s no one left to share the silence, no crowd to cheer, no king to honor. Just me and these hollowed stones, wondering if there’s anything left worth rebuilding—or if it’s time to let the dust take it all.
